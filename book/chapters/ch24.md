Two familiar groups and one that has no precedent. The last group is the
interesting one: five components that could not have been designed before
there was a second party that acts on the interface, and that every agentic
application ends up needing.

## Spatial

| Component | Composes | APG pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Canvas | `input.pointer`, `input.zoompan`, `surface.viewport` | (application) |
| Node graph | `input.dragdrop`, `input.selection`, `input.zoompan` | (application) |
| Bounding box editor | `input.pointer`, `input.zoompan`, `history.transaction` | (application) |
| Image annotation | `input.pointer`, `file.dropzone`, `file.export` | (application) |
| Minimap | `input.pointer`, `surface.viewport` | (decorative) |

**A spatial surface needs a fixed height**, which conflicts with letting the
frame grow. Pick a height that works at the narrowest width you accept, report
it once, and scroll or zoom inside it. A canvas that grows with its content
will fight `surface.resize` and lose.

**Pointer capture, `touch-action: none`, and zoom about the cursor.** All three
in Chapter 9, and all three are the difference between a canvas that feels
right and one that fights.

**A minimap is the cheapest orientation aid there is**, and the first thing
people miss when it is absent. Recipe 7 draws one into a 220 by 148 canvas in
eleven lines and it makes a graph twice as usable.

**Every gesture needs a button.** Zoom in, zoom out, reset, fit, and arrow
keys to pan. A canvas that can only be operated by dragging cannot be operated
at all by a significant number of people, and this is the group where that
failure is most common.

**Undo per gesture, not per frame.** A drag produces one transaction on
pointerup, not sixty during pointermove. Recipe 7 records the start position
on pointerdown and pushes a single transaction at the end.

## Media

| Component | Composes | APG pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Player transport | `media.playback`, `input.keyboard` | Toolbar, Slider |
| Scrubber | `media.playback`, `input.pointer` | Slider |
| Caption selector | `media.playback`, `input.keyboard` | Listbox |
| Thumbnail strip | `input.selection`, `input.keyboard` | Tabs, Listbox |

**Consider not replacing the native controls.** They handle keyboard, screen
readers, captions, picture-in-picture, playback rate and every platform's
conventions, and they do it in less code than your version. Replace them when
you need controls the native set does not have, and inherit the whole
accessibility contract when you do: Space to play, arrows to seek, a labelled
slider with `aria-valuetext` in minutes and seconds rather than in seconds,
and a caption toggle with `aria-pressed`.

**Pause when hidden.** Chapter 14. Audio continuing from a frame nobody can
see is genuinely alarming, and it is the fastest way to make a user close a
conversation.

**Fullscreen goes through the host.** `ui/request-display-mode`, not the
Fullscreen API, because `fullscreen` is not in the extension's permission set.
Chapter 16.

**A thumbnail strip is a tab list.** Give it roving tabindex, `aria-current`
on the selected item, and real labels rather than image alt text that says
"thumbnail".

## Agent-native

These five have no conventional web precedent. They exist because of Part III.

| Component | Composes | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Approval card | `approval.request`, `dialog.confirm`, `agent.contextUpdate` | Recipe 10 |
| Tool status chip | `tool.invoke`, `op.progress`, `op.cancel`, `op.error` | Recipes 9, 11 |
| Streaming log | `tool.partialOutput`, `input.selection`, `a11y.announce` | Recipe 9 |
| Context inspector | `agent.contextUpdate`, `agent.boundary` | Every demonstration |
| Proposal card | `ui.prefill`, `history.transaction`, `state.ownership` | Recipes 4, 7 |

**The approval card** is a decision surface with the six provenance fields
from Chapter 21 attached, and its whole design argument is that a card without
them trains people to click through it. Structurally it is a heading, a
consequence sentence, a description list, and two buttons. It is also where a
second confirmation belongs for high-risk actions, and where the audit trail is
exposed.

**The tool status chip** is a small object that represents one call: its name,
its state, its progress, and the controls that apply to it. The states are
queued, running, cancelled, failed and done, and each has a different set of
available actions. The chip is worth extracting as a component because
applications end up with several calls in flight, and a consistent
representation of "what is happening" is what makes that legible.

**The streaming log** is the append-only list from Chapter 23 with the
selection and follow behaviour that terminals taught everybody: follow the
tail until the user scrolls up, then stop following and say so. Recipe 9 turns
following off automatically when the user scrolls away from the bottom, which
is the behaviour nobody notices when it is right.

**The context inspector** shows what the view has published to the model. It
is a debugging tool that deserves to ship. A user who can see that the
assistant knows about three selected rows and nothing else can predict what it
will say, and the same panel is how a developer finds out that their view has
been publishing hover state for a week. Every demonstration in this book has
one, in the form of the "Show what the model was told" control.

**The proposal card** is the shape that makes agent editing safe. The agent's
version, visible next to the current one, with Accept and Decline, and
accepting recorded as one undo transaction. Chapters 18 and 20 both arrive at
it, from prefill and from conflict policy respectively, which is a decent sign
that it is the right primitive.

## Building the five, concretely

Each of the agent-native components has a small definition of done, and they
are worth stating because these are the components with no existing reference
to copy from.

**Approval card.** Renders a title, a risk level, a consequence sentence, and a
provenance list of at least four fields. Approve and Deny are both single
actions; high risk adds a confirmation. Every decision produces exactly one
context update carrying the outcome and nothing else. It never accepts an
approval over its own tool interface.

**Tool status chip.** Carries a name, one of five states, a progress value when
it has one, and exactly the controls that state allows. Queued has none.
Running has Cancel. Failed has Retry. Cancelled and Done have neither. The
state name is visible as text, not only as a colour.

**Streaming log.** Appends, trims to a cap, and never rebuilds. Follows the
tail until the user scrolls away, then stops and shows that it has stopped.
Selection is by identifier so it survives the next thousand lines.

**Context inspector.** Shows the last payload published to the model, verbatim,
and says when nothing has been published. It is one control and about ten
lines, and it is the fastest way to find out that a view has been leaking
hover state for a week.

**Proposal card.** Shows the agent's version next to what is currently there,
with Accept and Decline. Accepting is one undo transaction. The tool that
created it returns "proposed, not applied", so the agent does not report a
change that has not happened.

## The pattern behind all five

Every agent-native component makes an invisible thing visible. What the model
was told. What it is doing. What it is proposing. What it wants permission
for. What it has been told before.

That is the design brief for this whole layer, and it comes out of the same
constraint the rest of the book keeps hitting: the two parties cannot see each
other. The user cannot see the model's context. The model cannot see the
screen. Components that surface one to the other are the ones that make an
agentic interface feel like a place rather than a slot machine.

## Conformance checks

1. A spatial surface reports a stable height rather than growing with its
   content.
2. Every pointer gesture has a button or key equivalent.
3. A drag produces one undo transaction, not one per pointer event.
4. Media pauses when the frame is hidden.
5. Immersive playback goes through `ui/request-display-mode`.
6. An approval card renders provenance, and its decision produces exactly one
   context update.
7. A streaming log appends and trims, and never rebuilds its container.
8. Every agent-initiated change is announced once and is reversible.
