Chapter 7

Focus and Scroll

Inside the frame, focus and scrolling work exactly as they always have. Across the frame boundary, neither of them works at all, and the protocol has nothing to say about it.

Focus works normally inside your frame and does not cross its boundary in either direction. There is no message that makes it, in either the extension or core MCP, so a host cannot direct focus into your search field and you cannot hand it back to the composer when a form is finished. Two of the four capabilities here are plain web platform behaviour that needs nothing from anybody; the other two are gaps, and together they are the shortest illustration of exactly where this extension currently stops.

How does focus work inside my view?

Exactly as it always has. Elements are focusable, tabindex orders them, :focus-visible styles them, and <dialog> with showModal() traps focus and restores it on close. Nothing about the sandbox interferes, because focus is a property of your document and your document is entirely yours.

What changes is the consequences of getting it wrong. In a page, a user stuck in a focus trap can reach the address bar or the browser chrome. In a view they cannot: your frame is the whole world their keyboard can see, and the host’s own controls are on the other side of a boundary focus does not cross. Four things embedded views get wrong more often than pages do:

Manage a roving tabindex in composite widgets. A list of fifty rows should be one tab stop, not fifty. The focused row carries tabindex="0", every other row carries -1, and arrow keys move the zero. The selection lab in Chapter 9 does this in about ten lines. Without it, a keyboard user tabs through your entire dataset to reach the button after it.

Restore focus after overlays. Record what had focus before you open something, put it back when you close. The <dialog> element does this for you when you use showModal() and close(); anything hand-rolled does not.

Never trap focus outside a modal. A trap that survives its dialog is a keyboard user stuck inside your frame with no way out, and unlike a page they cannot press the address bar to escape.

Do not steal focus on load. Your view appears in the middle of somebody’s conversation, often while they are typing. Calling focus() during initialisation moves the caret out of the message box, and from the user’s point of view the application they did not ask for has just eaten a sentence.

Failure modes. Unsupported does not apply; the platform is always there. The realistic failures are a focus ring that the host’s stylesheet does not provide, which is why the view stylesheet in this repository sets :focus-visible explicitly rather than trusting an inherited default, and a focus ring clipped by an overflow: hidden container, which is a real accessibility failure that looks like a cosmetic one.

No. There is no message for focus in either direction, and the workarounds are worse than they look.

A host can call focus() on the iframe element, which gives the frame focus without choosing anything inside it and without telling your code that it happened. From your side the first thing you know is a focusin event on whatever the browser picked, usually the first tab stop, which is rarely the search field the user asked for. Press “Host tries to focus the view” in the demonstration and watch the transcript stay completely empty: nothing crossed the boundary, because there is nothing to cross it with.

The host cannot say “the user just asked to interact with your view, please focus your search field”. It can call focus() on the iframe element, which gives the frame focus without telling the document inside it anything useful, and without choosing what inside it should be focused. Press “Host tries to focus the view” in the demonstration and watch the transcript stay empty.

The view cannot say “I am done, please return focus to the composer”. After a user finishes with an embedded form, focus stays wherever the form left it, and the next keystroke goes to the frame rather than to the conversation.

Why it matters. Voice control and switch access both depend on being able to move focus deliberately across the whole surface a user perceives. From the user’s point of view the host window is one thing; the frame boundary is an implementation detail they did not agree to.

Workarounds. Two, both partial. A view can register a tool that focuses a named element, which gives an agent a way to do it even though the host has no direct route. And a view can avoid needing the handoff at all by not being the last step: finish the interaction with an explicit “Done” that sends ui/notifications/request-teardown, so the host removes the frame and focus returns to the host by ordinary means.

What a mechanism would look like. A ui/request-focus request from the view, with the host free to decline, and a ui/notifications/focus-granted notification in the other direction carrying an optional target selector. Both are cheap. Neither exists. The gap is filed in Appendix B.

Also ordinary platform behaviour: scrollIntoView, scrollTo, sticky headers, scroll-margin so a sticky header does not cover the thing you just scrolled to.

The one embedded-specific rule is about scroll containers. A view that lets its own body scroll competes with the host’s conversation scroller, and on a trackpad the result is scroll chaining that feels broken in both directions. Put the scroll on an inner container with a bounded height, and let the frame itself be exactly as tall as its content. This is also what makes surface.resize behave: a body that never scrolls has an honest scrollHeight.

Restoring position. Views are re-rendered. If your view has a long list, record the scroll offset alongside whatever state you persist, and restore it when the data comes back. Since state.save is a gap (Chapter 15), that usually means sending it to a server tool, which is heavier than it should be for a number.

You cannot. scrollIntoView moves your own scroll container; the conversation that contains your frame belongs to the host and is not reachable from inside an opaque origin.

This is the gap users actually notice, and it gets worse the longer a view lives. Your frame was rendered twenty messages ago and has scrolled well out of sight. Something happens inside it that needs a person: a tool call fails, a long job finishes, an approval lands in the queue. The view knows. The user is reading something else and will not find out until they scroll back, and nothing in the interface gives them a reason to.

Workarounds. If the host supports updateModelContext or message, the view can make the conversation itself move by putting something in it, but that is a heavy instrument for “please look over here” and it costs a turn. The realistic mitigation is to design for the view being invisible: assume nobody sees a change until they next look, make state legible on arrival rather than announcing it at the moment it happens, and use notify.banner for anything that must survive being missed.

What a mechanism would look like. A ui/request-reveal notification, with the host free to ignore it or to badge the collapsed view instead of scrolling to it. Chapter 8’s notify.attention is the same gap wearing a different hat, and a single mechanism would probably serve both.

A worked example: the escape hatch

Consider a view with a search field, inside a host with its own message composer. The user is typing in the composer, notices the view, clicks into the search field, types a query, and presses Enter. So far so ordinary.

Now they want to go back to the conversation. What are their options? Clicking works. Tabbing forward eventually leaves the frame, if the view has not trapped focus and if the remaining tab stops are few. Pressing Escape does nothing, because Escape is not defined as “leave this frame” anywhere, and the view has no way to hand focus back even if it wanted to.

For a mouse user this is invisible. For somebody driving by keyboard it is the difference between an application and an obstacle, and the reason the fix is not simply “add fewer tab stops” is that the view legitimately has controls.

What the recipes in Part V do about it is bound the number of tab stops with roving tabindex, never trap outside a modal, and end multi-step interactions with an explicit action that removes the view. That is a workaround for a missing message, and it is the best available answer today.

Conformance checks

  1. Tab from the host’s own controls reaches the view’s first focusable element and eventually leaves it.
  2. A modal opened inside the view traps focus and returns it on close.
  3. The focus ring is visible against the host’s background in both themes.
  4. Nothing in the view calls focus() before the user has interacted with it.
  5. Scrolling inside the view does not chain to the host’s conversation scroller while the view’s own scroller has room left.

The pattern worth carrying forward

Both gaps here are the same shape: a capability the view has inside itself that it cannot exercise at the boundary. It is worth carrying that shape into the rest of Part II, because it recurs. Drag and drop works inside the frame and stops at its edge. The clipboard can be written and not read. Dialogs can be drawn and not raised above the host. In every case the platform gives the view a local version of something, and the boundary is where the local version runs out.

That is not an accident or an oversight. Every one of those boundaries is a place where a hostile view could reach into the host’s window, and the extension’s answer so far has been to leave the boundary closed and add explicit messages one at a time where the value is clear. ui/open-link and ui/download-file are two that made it through. The entries in Appendix B are the ones still waiting.
